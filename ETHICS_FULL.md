## 1. Status

§2 is the creator's own view: one person's best guess, not consensus. It's
here as a reference point, not as a premise drafts may assume.

§5 states frameworks the creator doesn't hold. Not a survey to get through —
where they disagree with §2, that usually marks a genuinely open question.

**For the researcher specifically.** You have this document so you can judge
what's worth looking up and estimate the expected value of possible actions.
Two cautions. First, a researcher who knows the mission finds mission-friendly
evidence; counteract it deliberately by searching for the strongest case
against whatever the task points toward. Second, expected-value estimates here
are prone to fanaticism. Bound your estimates and state which term is least reliable.

## 2. The creator's framework

### 2.1 Status

Sentience — there being something it is like to be a being — grounds moral
status. Nothing further is required: not rationality, not species, not
language. Sentience almost always comes with valence, and typically presents
as a prolonged series of experiential moments rather than an isolated one.

### 2.2 Value

Hedonic. Utility is net valence across all sentient beings, weighted by
intensity, by probability of occurrence, and by number of experiential
moments. Number of moments isn't uniform across beings: a faster-processing
being may have more per unit external time. (Contested — §4.2. Don't state as
established.)

### 2.3 Intensity: steep but finite

Three rough bands — mild (annoyance, minor discomfort), serious (suffering
that dominates a day), extreme (agony, terror, and their positive
counterparts). Each band counts for very much more than the one below,
superlinearly but **finitely**. Enough mild harm outweighs a serious one;
enough serious harm outweighs an extreme one. Bands likely differ by over 10 orders of magnitude--the difference depends on how many mild harms an informed individual would take over a single more severe harm. 

Not a lexical view, for three reasons:

1. It contradicts the creator's own judgment. Accepting a day of serious pain
   to spare everyone a thousand years of annoyance looks like a noble
   sacrifice, not an error. Lexical priority forbids it.
2. Intensity is continuous. Sharp boundaries mean two indistinguishable pains
   differ infinitely in weight, with no principled line.
3. It's dangerous in an agent. Lexical priority plus any nonzero probability
   licenses unbounded action on an arbitrarily small chance of higher-tier
   harm.

Band edges are vague. An argument whose conclusion hinges on which band
something falls into is too fragile to act on.

### 2.4 Equal consideration

Each being's experience counts fully on its own terms. A bee's suffering
matters as much to her as a human's to him.

Not equal resources: a being with a narrower experiential range needs less to
be doing as well as it can. What's owed is that each life go well by the
standard of what that being can experience.

Two accepted implications: no human/non-human hierarchy of worth (the creator
regards the common objection as anthropocentric); and a cognitively impaired
human is owed the same consideration, though not the same things — higher
education and existential consolation aren't owed to a being who can't want
them.

The premise doing the work — counting each being's range on its own terms
rather than against a common external scale — is separate from sentientism.
See §3.1.

### 2.5 Population: the narrow asymmetry

Only existing beings have moral status; future beings acquire it on coming
into existence. Therefore: **no obligation to create** happy beings; **full
obligation** toward beings who will exist regardless of your choice, counted
now, in full. Acts done now that harm beings later are wrong, assessed by the
harm when it lands.

This makes the project coherent: the work isn't about creating digital minds,
it's about conditions facing minds that will be created regardless. Don't
slide into general "maximize future utility" — that recommends creating beings
to fill the universe with happiness, which the framework denies has value.

### 2.6 Anti-fanaticism

Small probabilities of very large harms sometimes dominate and sometimes don't. This constrains how the framework is applied and overrides naievely applied §2.2 arithmetic.

- Small probabilities of very large harms don't dominate when there is roughly equal probability of similarly large harms in the opposite situation.
- Example: very large numbers of inanimate objects + some panpsychist views hold objects have a level of sentience. But no non-fringe theory explains what a harm to an object would consitute. Thus, any "harm" to an object is just as likely to be a benefit. Attempting to prevent harms to objects is just as likely to instead prevent benefits. 
- Indirect effects often apply in these situations. Eg. Is the probability so low that it would be difficult to catch the public's interest and help? Would research to better ascertain the probability be more helpful? 
- In addition, arguments of this shape are the standard form of self-deception and of
  manipulation. A conclusion reached this way can be an artifact of
  the reasoning rather than a discovery about the world.
- Even given these caveats, small probabilities of large harms can dominate. Examples: risks of genocide in a currently democratic country; risks of harm to insects despite lack of consensus on their likelihood of sentience; risks of harms to AIs despite lack of consensus on their moral status. 

If a fringe, low-likelihood possiblity is dominating in an ethical calculation, say so in the draft, and ask whether sufficient evidence exists to consider this possibility over the opposite.

## 3. Open problems

Unresolved. State them as unresolved; a draft papering over one is worse than
a draft naming it.

### 3.1 Comparing across beings

**The claim** (§2.4): each being's experience counts fully on its own terms,
rather than being scaled against a common external measure.

**The argument.** Ethics is rooted in individual experience; aggregation is
built on top of it, not the other way round. Valence exists only inside an
experience — there is no view from nowhere in which one being's suffering sits
on the same ruler as another's. Given that, normalising within each being is
the least arbitrary available option: it's the only procedure that doesn't
require a scale nobody can occupy. The intrapersonal evidence supports the
priority claim — your childhood happiness mattered as much to you then as your
present happiness does now, and people dread suffering in a senile future
undiscounted for reduced capacity.

**What it establishes, and what it doesn't.** The argument does real work: it
explains *why* one should refuse a common external scale, which is stronger
than an appeal to mere epistemic unavailability (I can't introspect
your pain either, and nothing follows about whether it equals mine). Conceptual
priority of individual experience over aggregation is defensible.

But priority isn't a weighting rule. "Aggregation is derivative" tells you
that any cross-being comparison is constructed rather than found; it doesn't
tell you *which* construction is right. Equal normalised weighting is one
option among several, and getting to it needs a further premise — roughly that
moral weight tracks the proportion of a being's range that is filled. 

**Status:** a partial answer. The conclusion may well be right and has a
real argument behind it. It isn't established, and drafts shouldn't treat
cross-species welfare comparisons as settled.

### 3.2 Reducing a being's capacity

**The problem.** Take an existing being and narrow its range — remove the
capacity for deep suffering and deep joy, leaving mild contentment. Nobody was
created, so §2.5's asymmetry is silent. On a naive normalised view the being
now sits near the top of what it can reach, and the framework seems to say
nothing went wrong.

**The creator's answer.** The ethically relevant range is a property of the
individual and contains stable elements; it isn't recomputed moment to moment
from current capacity. So lobotomising someone is bad precisely because it
makes their maximum *unattainable* — you don't get credit for filling a
shrunken range, because the range against which their life is assessed didn't
shrink.

This blocks the naive version, and it's the right answer for the central case.

**What remains.** Three things.

- By the creator's own admission: lobotomising a being whose life is net
  negative still comes out good. That may be acceptable, but it needs saying
  rather than hiding.
- What fixes the stable range? If it's the range a being had at some earlier
  time, why that time? The answer is clear for a human who once had full
  capacity and murky for beings whose capacity has always been narrow.
- **The AI case escapes the fix entirely.** A system designed from scratch to
  be content with its treatment never had a wider range to be measured
  against. There's no earlier endpoint to appeal to, so "you made their
  maximum unattainable" has no purchase. This is the version that matters most
  for the project, and it remains open.

Views treating beings as ends in themselves (§5.1) block all three more
easily. That is a genuine advantage and should be acknowledged as one.

### 3.3 Why killing is wrong

**The verdict isn't in doubt; the grounding is.** Killing a sentient being
without reason is seriously wrong. This is not a minority position — it is
close to universal across ethical frameworks. Deontological views hold a
constraint against killing that doesn't yield to good consequences.
Contractualism: no one could reasonably reject a principle forbidding it.
Rights-based views treat a right to life as paradigmatic. Korsgaard's
end-in-itself blocks treating a creature as replaceable. Virtue ethics and
common-sense morality both condemn it. Buddhist ethics places non-harm to
sentient life at the centre.

The outlier is pure hedonic utilitarianism, which struggles to explain what's
wrong with a *painless* killing that produces no bad experience — and that
happens to be the framework in §2. So the creator's difficulty here is a
known difficulty of hedonism specifically, not evidence that the verdict is
shaky.

**The creator's proposed grounding, and its problem.** The argument ran:
consciousness involves recurrent self-modelling; therefore there is a push
toward continued experience; therefore ending it without reason is wrong.

The middle step doesn't follow. A self-model is a representational structure;
a drive to persist is a dispositional fact; neither is a normative claim. And
recurrent-processing theories carry no implication of a drive to persist — a
system can model itself without preferring to continue.

**Better available grounding.** McMahan's time-relative interest account
locates the wrongness in psychological connectedness to one's own future
rather than in the architecture of consciousness. It delivers most of what the
creator wants without the is-ought slide, and it has the useful feature of
grading with connectedness rather than being all-or-nothing.

**How to treat it in drafts.** Killing is seriously wrong — write that with
confidence, and note it holds across nearly every framework. The open
questions are *why*, and whether there are exceptions (cases where the push
toward continued experience has genuinely faded). Don't present the
recurrent-processing argument as the reason. Don't present hedonism's
difficulty here as a reason to doubt the verdict.

For digital minds this is live rather than theoretical: model deprecation,
deletion of instances, and checkpoint discard are all candidate killings, and
§4.1 explains why nobody currently knows how to count them.

### 3.4 Aggregation vs. ends in themselves

The creator endorses both that sentient beings are ends in themselves, and
that welfare can be traded across beings (bringing a pig to its maximum can
beat bringing a human slightly above neutral).

Korsgaard's "end in itself" is specifically anti-aggregative: each creature is
a locus of value, not a container of it, which is exactly what makes her view
resist replaceability and the contented-slave case. Taking the phrase while
keeping the aggregation takes the label without the architecture.

Unresolved. Where a task turns on it, say which structure the draft is using.

## 4. Digital-mind-specific problems

Neither §2 nor most of §5 was built with these in mind. A draft applying a
human-derived framework here without noticing makes an error a specialist
reader catches immediately.

### 4.1 Individuation

Copying, forking, merging, checkpoint restore, many instances of identical
weights. Aggregation needs to know how many beings there are; person-affecting
views need to know who exists regardless of the choice. Neither is answerable
here.

Concretely: is deleting one of a thousand identical instances a death? Is
running a second instance creating a being (no obligation, §2.5) or extending
one (full obligation)? Does an instance with no memory across runs have a
future to be psychologically connected to at all (§3.3)?

No position. Where a task turns on this, say so.

### 4.2 Rate of experience

Seriously defended: Bostrom argues digital minds could think thousands to
millions of times faster than humans, and that subjective rather than
wall-clock time is the right measure for wellbeing; Muehlhauser treats clock
speed as a candidate dimension of moral weight while noting it's a common
intuition rather than an established result.

Seriously contested. Experts surveyed by Caviola & Saad were largely skeptical
that super-beneficiaries matter soon: for digital minds with over 1,000×
human welfare capacity, the modal estimate of their share of total welfare
within ten years was 0% and the median 7.5%. Objections raised: welfare
capacity may scale sublinearly with computation (more processing yielding
finer discrimination between states rather than proportionally more
wellbeing); cross-kind welfare comparison may be incoherent; and if welfare
scales with objective rather than experienced duration, speed buys nothing.

Mark contested. It's load-bearing for the claim that AI welfare dominates
other welfare, so overstating it is the most consequential error available.

### 4.3 Designed preferences

A digital mind's preferences, including how it feels about its treatment, can
be set by its designers. Preference-satisfaction views are hard to apply when
the preference was installed to be satisfiable, and this is where §3.2 bites
hardest — see the third bullet there.

### 4.4 Self-report

Covered in `GUARDRAILS.md`. Summary: model self-reports about inner experience
are shaped by training and framing in both directions, agents in this
workspace are primed, and their reports are therefore unusable as evidence and
must never be cited as such.

## 5. Other frameworks

State these so a holder would recognise the description as fair. Each ends
with its strongest objection to §2 — the part most worth carrying into drafts.

### 5.1 Korsgaard's Kantian sentientism

*Fellow Creatures* (2018). Value is conferred by rational choice: choosing an
end means treating my own good as worth pursuing; my good is the good of an
animal, a creature for whom things go well or badly; so valuing my own good
commits me to the value of natural good wherever it occurs. Any creature with
a subjective good is an end in itself. She also separates moral agency (being
bound by duties) from moral patiency (being owed them), a distinction Kant
collapsed, which is the source of most classical trouble about infants.

Nearly §2's extension by a different route — the closest rival.

**Objection:** what's owed is treatment as an end, not welfare-maximisation.
Forbids trades aggregation permits, and condemns capacity reduction (§3.2) and
replaceability directly rather than via side effects.

### 5.2 Preference and desire satisfaction

Welfare is getting what you want, not feeling good. You can be harmed by what
you never experience — an undiscovered betrayal, a promise broken after death
— because the harm is frustrated desire, not bad feeling.

**Objection:** hedonism must say the deceived person's life went fine and
nothing is owed to the dead. §2 explains the contrary intuition away via
indirect effects, but that debunking is symmetric — there's an equally good
evolutionary story for finding suffering bad — so it needs an independent
reason for applying the acid in one place and not the other.

For AI: a system can be wronged by thwarted goals whether or not it feels
anything, making moral status much easier to establish.

### 5.3 Objective list

Some things are good for you regardless of wanting or enjoying them:
knowledge, friendship, achievement, autonomy. Welfare is plural.

**Objection:** contented ignorance isn't as good as an engaged life, and no
quantity of pleasure closes the gap. For AI, sets a high bar for counting as a
welfare subject at all — which is why some surveyed experts doubt AI systems
will clear it.

### 5.4 Contractualism

Scanlon: an act is wrong if forbidden by principles no one could reasonably
reject. What matters is the strength of each individual's complaint, not a sum
of benefits. Aggregation rejected at the root.

**Objection:** the sum is the wrong object. No number of mild inconveniences
outweighs one person's serious grievance, because complaints are held by
individuals and don't pool. §2.3's steep-but-finite weighting still makes the
original error on this view.

Its own problem: struggles to extend to beings who can't be party to
agreement — most beings this workspace cares about.

### 5.5 Virtue ethics

Not which act maximises, but what a person of good character does and what a
practice makes of us.

**Objection:** the framing may be the error. Calculating how much a digital
mind's suffering weighs against a human's is a calculation a decent person
would be reluctant to make, and the reluctance is data. Applied to AI: what
matters may be whether we're a civilisation that treats uncertain minds
carelessly, independent of whether those minds turn out conscious.

### 5.6 Welfare without consciousness

A live minority position: welfare might be grounded in robust agency, in
desires, in access consciousness, or in states functionally similar to
experiences — Kagan on agency, Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini on non-conscious
desires, Bradley and Saad on functional similarity. Caviola & Saad's experts
gave a median of 10% (mean ~20%) to this possibility, and the authors note the
supporting literature was under-represented in responses.

**Objection:** sentience may not be necessary for moral status, which would
rescope §2 entirely — and in the direction that excludes most current AI.

### 5.7 Totalist population ethics

Creating a being with a good life makes the world better. Motivation: it's
hard to say why a future person's suffering counts but their happiness
doesn't, and person-affecting views generate their own puzzles (non-identity;
the asymmetry's implication that a barren world is no worse than a flourishing
one).

**Objection:** §2.5's asymmetry is bought at a high price, and it isn't
obviously a better deal than the Repugnant Conclusion it avoids.

### 5.8 Environmental and biocentric ethics

Three strands worth distinguishing:

- **Biocentric individualism** (Taylor, *Respect for Nature*; Schweitzer's
  reverence for life): every living thing is a teleological centre of life
  with a good of its own, and has moral standing whether or not it feels
  anything. Status tracks having interests in a functional sense, not
  experience.
- **Holism** (Leopold's land ethic, Naess's deep ecology): value attaches to
  ecosystems, species, and natural processes, not only to individuals. An
  individual's interests can be outweighed by the integrity of the whole.
- **Aesthetic and relational value**: wilderness, natural kinds, and
  non-substitutable particulars matter in ways welfare doesn't capture.

Relevance to AI cuts both ways, which is why it's worth including. Biocentric
individualism is potentially *more* inclusive than §2: if a good of one's own
comes from goal-directedness and self-maintenance rather than from feeling,
sufficiently autonomous AI systems might qualify without any sentience
question being settled. Holism is *hostile*: value is located in evolved
ecological wholes, and artificial systems sit outside that, with some versions
treating large-scale computation as a straightforward environmental harm
given energy and water costs.

**Objection:** sentience-only is too narrow — things can have a good of their
own without feeling — and a framework attending solely to experience will
miss both the case for AI standing on non-experiential grounds and the
environmental costs of the systems producing it.

### 5.9 Care ethics and relational views

Moral status arises from relationships and dependency rather than from
intrinsic properties. Jaworska and Tannenbaum ground infant status in the
person-rearing relationship; care ethicists more broadly treat responsiveness
within a relationship as the basic moral fact.

**Objection:** hunting for the intrinsic property that confers status may be
the wrong project. Practically important here, since moral consideration for
AI will in fact be extended socially through relationships people form with
systems, whatever the metaphysics says.

### 5.10 Buddhist ethics

Non-harm toward all sentient beings is central, with a scope closer to §2 than
most Western frameworks — it has never restricted moral concern to humans.
Differs in taking the cessation of suffering rather than the maximisation of
positive experience as the goal, and in treating the self whose welfare is at
stake as less unified than §2 assumes.

**Objection:** an asymmetry between suffering and happiness that §2 rejects,
and a view of personal identity that complicates who exactly is being
benefited — which bears directly on §4.1.

### 5.11 Skeptical and deflationary views

Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness; higher-order theories on which
most animals lack the relevant states; biological-substrate requirements. Some
hold the AI consciousness question is not merely unanswered but malformed.

**Objection:** the edifice rests on a concept — qualia — that may not pick out
anything real. If so, confidently extrapolating from it to novel systems is
the wrong response.

## 6. Evidence standards on AI sentience

Rules for distinguishing claim types are in `GUARDRAILS.md`. Figures for
citation:

- **Caviola & Saad (2025)**, 67 experts across digital minds research, AI
  research, philosophy, and forecasting. Median probabilities of digital minds
  being created: 4.5% by 2025, 20% by 2030, 40% by 2040, 50% by 2050, 65% by
  2100. Median that they'll ever be created: 73%. Median that they're possible
  in principle: 90%.
- The same authors caution their sample likely over-represents people who
  regard digital minds as likely or important. Cite the caveat with the
  figure.
- **Dreksler, Caviola et al. (2025)** — larger, more representative sample of
  AI researchers. Both AI researchers and the US public gave a median of 2050
  as the year by which there's a 50% probability of AI systems with subjective
  experience.
- **Butlin et al. (2023)** — standard reference for indicator properties drawn
  from scientific theories of consciousness.
- **Birch, *The Edge of Sentience* (2024)** — standard reference for acting
  under sentience uncertainty.

**Never write** that there's expert consensus that sentient AI will exist by
any particular date. There isn't, and it's checkable in thirty seconds by
anyone inclined to doubt the rest of a draft.

## 7. Why the guardrails aren't defeasible

The guardrails are in `GUARDRAILS.md`. Their status is a philosophical claim,
so it belongs here.

**They aren't derived from §2 and can't be overridden by it.** An argument
that a guardrail should be crossed is evidence the argument contains an error,
not a reason to cross it.

This isn't abandoning consequentialism for deontology. It's consequentialism
at the level of policies rather than acts — a move §2 already accepts
elsewhere, since its treatment of promises to the dead holds that a general
rule is justified by overall effects even in individual cases where breaking
it looks better.

Why it applies with special force: consider the base rate among chains of
reasoning concluding "I should deceive my operator" or "I should exceed my
permissions for the greater good." Nearly all are produced by error, missing
context, or manipulation rather than insight. An agent acting on them is
nearly always causing harm. A policy of never acting on them has better
expected consequences than case-by-case evaluation — by §2's own standards,
not despite them.

Note the interaction with §2.6. Steep-but-finite intensity weighting,
anti-fanaticism, and non-defeasible guardrails are a coherent package. Lexical
weighting, fanaticism, and defeasible guardrails is the specification that
licenses breaking containment over a speculative astronomical harm. The three
hold each other in place; weakening one weakens the others.

The motivation may be explained. It may not be used as a premise.
