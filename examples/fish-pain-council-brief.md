# Briefing note: Do fish feel pain, and what does it mean for the children's fishing competition?

**For:** Council Member ______ · **Prepared:** ______

## Bottom line

Scientists disagree about whether fish consciously *feel* pain, and that won't be resolved before the council votes. Both sides do agree on one thing: hooking injures fish, stresses them and kills some of them, and those harms count. Where they split is whether fish should get "the benefit of the doubt" about pain. That gives the council a two-part decision:

- **Running the event with strict handling rules** is justified on either view of fish pain.
- **Ending the event** depends on giving fish the benefit of the doubt. Some scientists call this responsible; others call it "dogma." That part is a values judgment for the council, not a scientific finding.

**Ask staff before voting:** Are fish kept or released? About how many are caught each year? Is the lake stocked for the event, and by whom? Who co-sponsors it, and how many families attend?

## What the science says

- **Fish detect injury.** Rainbow trout have nociceptors around the mouth, where a hook goes in. These are nerve endings that respond to tissue damage. When researchers put irritants on trout lips, the fish behaved abnormally and took longer to start feeding again (Sneddon et al. 2003). Another lab didn't reproduce some of these behavioural findings (Newby & Stevens 2008, as reported by Rose et al. 2014). That replication used unanaesthetised fish and was itself disputed.
- **Whether fish *feel* it is contested.** Skeptics argue that detecting injury is not the same as feeling pain. Fish lack the cortex regions associated with pain in humans, and fish often resume normal feeding soon after surgery. Skeptics conclude fish are "unlikely to experience pain" (Rose et al. 2014; Key 2016). Researchers such as Lynne Sneddon and Culum Brown reply that the evidence as a whole supports pain or sentience in fish (Issues forum 2020).
- **The disagreement is over precaution, not over harm.** The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness finds "at least a realistic possibility" of conscious experience in fish and calls ignoring it "irresponsible." Two welfare scientists who doubt many common pain tests still say fish "should be treated as if sentient" (Mason & Lavery 2022). The skeptics' paper rejects "benefit of the doubt" policy. Yet its authors still back welfare measured by injury, stress and survival (Rose et al. 2014). One of those co-authors describes himself as "a fish pain skeptic, but a fish welfare advocate" (Cooke 2020).

## What this means for the competition

**Released fish don't all survive.** Across 252 estimates, mostly from freshwater fish, the median share of released fish that died was 7.8% (Hühn & Arlinghaus 2011). Rates were higher with deep hooking, natural bait and warm water (Bartholomew & Bohnsack 2005). If the event keeps its catch, every fish caught dies, and these figures don't apply.

**Options:**

1. **Keep the event, with welfare rules.** These could include:
   - barbless hooks
   - adult volunteers who unhook fish quickly, keeping them in or near the water
   - a cap on fish kept per child
   - cancelling the event if the water is above a set temperature

   This option holds up whoever is right about pain. It reduces harm but doesn't remove it.
2. **Replace it with a no-hook "lake day".** This could include fish watching, dip-netting for insects or a habitat clean-up. Children still get outdoors, but no fish are hooked. Some families who come for the fishing may not attend.
3. **Discontinue it.** This removes the risk to fish. It also ends an event some residents value, and anglers are often active supporters of local waters.

On the evidence, some form of option 1 is the minimum. Whether to go further is a judgment about how much weight an unresolved risk of pain deserves against the event's benefits.

## Notes for the operator (not part of the brief)

**Expected value.** *Direct:* this affects the fish caught at one annual event. I don't know the scale. A guess is a few dozen to a few hundred fish a year. Among released fish, a few percent to a fifth may die, and more if the event is catch-and-keep. Any pain from hooking and handling lasts minutes to hours per fish. If the event was stocked, ending it could also mean fewer fish are raised and moved for it (a guess; I didn't check how this lake is managed). *Indirect, and more speculative:*
- A council openly applying "realistic possibility → precaution" to fish creates a small local precedent that other municipalities or school programmes could cite.
- Children's events shape norms. A no-hook "lake day" may teach that fish are worth protecting. Ending the event could also provoke backlash among local anglers, who are often among the strongest supporters of lake conservation (Cooke 2020 and Porcher 2020 in the Issues forum make related points). That backlash could cost goodwill for future animal-welfare asks.
- Option 2 (welfare rules) may gain more support than outright cancellation and still help fish at many future events if other towns copy it.

On balance, this is a small direct effect with modest, uncertain precedent value. It's worth doing well, but it's not a high-leverage use of advocacy capital unless the council's decision would be publicised.

**Uncertainty.**
- The pain debate is genuinely open. The claim that both sides agree the measurable harms count, and split over precaution, is my synthesis. It rests on Rose et al. 2014's own text, but Key's position is more radical: he holds that fish may have no awareness at all, per Mason & Lavery. I didn't check whether Key endorses function-based welfare.
- The brief says "some form of option 1 is the minimum." That is a mild recommendation, not neutral analysis. Cut it if the council member wants a strictly neutral brief.
- The welfare rules in option 1 are common-sense inferences from the mortality risk factors (hook location, bait, temperature, handling). I didn't confirm them in the full text of an angling-practice guideline. Cooke & Sneddon 2007 lists terminal tackle, air exposure, handling and extreme conditions as levers, but I saw only its headings and snippets.
- I cut an earlier claim that children handle fish longer than adults, because it had no source.
- The mortality figures pool many species and settings and may not reflect this lake's species, water temperature or event format.
- The 614 signatory count for the NY Declaration is from the declaration's site as fetched today. Early press reported 39 initial signatories. I left the number out of the brief.
- I read Rose et al. 2014's abstract and key sections in full. Key 2016 is characterised only through secondary sources. Newby & Stevens 2008 is reported only as Rose et al. describe it. I fetched the Sneddon 2003 paper only as an abstract.
- In checking, the verifier attributed the Cooke quote to Arlinghaus. I re-checked the page: letters there are signed at the end, and the quote is in Cooke's letter.
- I assumed a US/Commonwealth-style council with no local legal constraints. I didn't check local law.
